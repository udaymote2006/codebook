2 · Course spine

One question runs through Prof. Ranjan's half. His slides pose it as "How can we computationally represent the structure of language?" (slide, InfoTheory deck, slide 3). His answer arrives in three steps. First, language is structured, so it is predictable: Zipf and Shannon's redundancy. Second, predictability can be measured: entropy, conditional entropy, MI, KL, and finally perplexity. Third, structure can be modelled as probabilities over sequences (n-grams) or over hidden tags (HMMs), or drawn as weighted finite-state machines. His closing answer: "One is probability, another is finite-state machine" (R6 01:24:50).

Prof. Varshney's half takes the same next-word problem and replaces counts with learned vectors. One-hot vectors and a lookup matrix lead to word2vec (predictive), then GloVe (count-based), then the limits of word vectors (fastText, bias, multilingual). From there she moves to neural LMs and RNNs. Her n-gram LM in D4 is the bridge back to R6–R7.

The link to Part 1 is that both halves score outputs with cross-entropy and softmax. In vision these scored class labels; here they score the next word.

Definitions in his words

  • in class "Language does not directly represent the external world. It represents our conceptualized experience or thoughts." R1 00:59:33

Worked example

R4 · Zipf's law, Shannon's approximations, entropy

Prof. Ranjan · Fri 25 Sep · Tactiq live only (1 h 35 m; no video in folder) · slides: InfoTheory 1–39 · Notion ✓

  • f ∝ 1/r
  • least effort
  • efficiency vs robustness
  • monkey typing
  • nth-order approximation
  • redundancy ≈ 50%
  • 2.3 bits/char

Problem → idea

Word frequencies are not normally distributed: a few words are very frequent and most are rare. Can this regularity be quantified? This leads to Zipf, then Shannon, then entropy.

  • in class "The frequency is inversely proportional to rank. So the multiplication of frequency and rank is constant." R4 00:09:54
  • in class Why Zipfian: the principle of least effort (frequent words are short), and the efficiency–robustness trade-off. Short words are cheap but easily misheard; long words carry more information and survive noise. R4 00:09:54
  • in class In the monkey-typing experiment, random text also gives Zipf-like curves. "Human language is not random… you can predict the next word given the prior context," so more research is needed: the "Zipf mystery". R4 00:14:27, 00:18:29
  • in class Raising the n-gram order makes output look more like English, but "if you keep doing it, say 7-gram, 8-gram, this approach fumbles", because long sequences are rarely seen. R4 00:49:26
Exam favourite · slide 11 question

"Should the 50th most common word occur with three times the frequency of the 150th?" Yes. f·r = const, so f₅₀/f₁₅₀ = 150/50 = 3. The slide also says the most frequent word occurs twice as often as the second (1/1 ÷ 1/2 = 2).

R5 · Joint and conditional entropy, chain rule, MI, KL

Prof. Ranjan · Sat 26 Sep · Tactiq live only (1 h 27 m; no video) · slides: InfoTheory 37–52 · Notion ✓

Trap corrected in class

"Entropy, by default, does not truly represent randomness in the sequence… it's just talking about randomness of the event under study." Language depends on history, so you need conditional entropy. R5 00:15:26

Asked in class, accepted answer: "It doesn't say anything about the sequences." "Very nice." R5 01:11:50

Derivations he said to know

Conditional entropy (he did it on the board and said "this is the derivation that I want you guys to really understand", R5 00:28:12):

H(Y|X) = Σₓ p(x)·H(Y|X=x) = −Σₓ p(x) Σᵧ p(y|x) log p(y|x) = −Σₓ,ᵧ p(x,y) log p(y|x)

The last step uses p(x)·p(y|x) = p(x,y). Umbrella example from the slide: X ∈ {Sunny, Rainy}, so H(Y|X) = p(Sunny)·H(Y|Sunny) + p(Rainy)·H(Y|Rainy).

Chain rule (R5 00:34:44): log of a product is a sum of logs; split the expectation.

H(X,Y) = −E[log p(x,y)] = −E[log p(x) + log p(y|x)] = H(X) + H(Y|X) = H(Y) + H(X|Y)

Mutual information and its symmetry asked (R5 01:11:52):

I(X;Y) = Σₓ,ᵧ p(x,y) log [ p(x,y) / (p(x)p(y)) ]

Proof of symmetry: swap the names X and Y. The joint p(x,y) = p(y,x) is the same table, and the denominator p(x)p(y) is a product, so it is unchanged. Hence I(X;Y) = I(Y;X). Equivalent route: I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y) by the chain rule, and this last form is symmetric in X and Y. If X and Y are independent, p(x,y) = p(x)p(y), so log 1 = 0 and I = 0.

  • slide MI is "the part of uncertainty that the two variables share" (Venn diagram). Examples: studying and passing; P(Delhi | New) > P(Delhi), which is how MI finds collocations. R5 01:04:03
  • in class KL divergence (relative entropy) between p and q is "the average number of bits that are wasted by encoding events from distribution p with a code based on the not-quite-right distribution q". Both distributions must be over the same event space, "else it will be apples and oranges". R5 01:06:37

Student Q&A

in class Q (Abhishek Kumar): Mathematically H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X) = H(Y) + H(X|Y), but why does total joint uncertainty not depend on which variable you describe first? A: Think of the joint as a "mental bag" where order does not matter; you impose the order when you choose to say one thing first, because you can say only one thing at a time. R5 01:21:54–01:25:17

The one sentence to memorise

Conditional entropy is the average leftover uncertainty after seeing the context. The chain rule adds it to H(X), and MI is how much the context removed.

R6 · Entropy on language, perplexity, n-grams, Markov chains

Prof. Ranjan · Sat 3 Oct · Tactiq live (1 h 56 m) · video probably Lec6 (not confirmed) · slides: InfoTheory 53–63 · Notion ✓

  • pointwise conditional entropy
  • total surprisal
  • Ĥ = H_total / N
  • PP = 2^Ĥ
  • Markov assumption
  • bigram MLE
  • weighted FSA

Definitions

  • in class "A language model is nothing but a probability distribution over language." Training assumes the corpus is representative of English. R6 00:33:50
  • slide Pointwise conditional entropy (surprisal of the next word w given history h under model m): −logm P(w|h). Total surprisal = sum over the sentence (chain rule). Ĥ = H_total/N is the average surprise per word.
  • in class Log base sets the unit: base 2 gives bits. R6 00:35:33
  • in class "Perplexity is nothing but 2 to the power of H." It is "the number of possible next words that can follow any word". Lower perplexity means a better model that makes more confident predictions. R6 00:38:16, 00:43:14
Trap

Entropy and perplexity are related (PP = 2^H) but "capturing different essence". Entropy is the expected surprise, or the minimum bits to transmit. Perplexity is the effective number of choices per word. R6 00:47:20–00:47:55

n-gram estimation

Bigram: P(wₙ | wₙ₋₁) = C(wₙ₋₁ wₙ) / C(wₙ₋₁) Trigram: P(coffee | put the) = C(put the coffee) / C(put the)

slide Unigram: P(S) = P(He)·P(put)·P(the)… Bigram for "He put the coffee on the table": P(put|He)·P(the|put)·P(coffee|the)·P(on|coffee)·P(the|on)·P(table|the). An n-gram model is a Markov model (Markov chain).

asked From the Markov-chain diagram, what is the probability of the sequence "he is"? The class answered 0.6, and he confirmed it. R6 01:22:12

Disputed in class

A student said the FSA was incorrect because of a self-loop ("in compilers we never…"). He replied that a self-loop is allowed: "you can have a language where it's repeating", as in "he, he is my brother". He took the remaining discussion offline. R6 01:24:25–01:25:47 For the exam: a weighted FSA or Markov chain may have self-loops.

The one sentence to memorise

An LM assigns probability by the chain rule, an n-gram truncates the history (the Markov assumption), and perplexity 2^Ĥ counts how many words the model is still choosing between.

HMM = probabilistic Moore machine

  • slide Start probability πᵢ; transition P(j|i), e.g. P(N|N); emission P(o|i), e.g. P(British|N). Outgoing transitions from each state sum to 1, and emissions from each state sum to 1.
  • slide Example: P(A|A) = ⅓, P(N|A) = ⅔, P(A|N) = 1/10, P(N|N) = 9/10. Each row sums to 1 ✓.
  • slide HMM factorisation: P(N)·P(British|N)·P(N|N)·P(Left|N)·P(V|N)·P(Waffles|V)·…
score(tags) = π(t₁)·b_t₁(w₁) · Πₜ a(tₜ₋₁→tₜ)·b_tₜ(wₜ) best = argmax over all qᵀ tag sequences

The one sentence to memorise

An HMM scores a tag path as start × emissions × transitions, and Viterbi finds the best of the qᵀ paths in O(T·q²) by reusing the best partial path to each state.

GloVe

  • in class A co-occurrence matrix grows with the vocabulary, is very sparse and high-dimensional, so reduce dimensions (to about 25–1000). D3 00:31:36
  • in class Ice/steam example: the ratio P(k|ice)/P(k|steam) is ≫ 1 when k is associated with ice (solid), ≪ 1 for steam (gas), and ≈ 1 when k does not distinguish them (water). D3 00:36:10, 00:42:44

The one sentence to memorise

Negative sampling makes word2vec cheap by classifying a true pair against K sampled noise pairs. GloVe instead fits dot products to log co-occurrence counts, because ratios of co-occurrence carry meaning.

8 · Flashcards and mock quiz

Flashcards

PP in terms of H

PP = 2^Ĥ, with Ĥ = H_total/N (bits). SRILM: 10^(−logprob/N).

Entropy of a fair n-sided die

log₂ n bits (8 sides: 3 bits).

Chain rule of entropy

H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X) = H(Y) + H(X|Y).

I(X;Y) three ways

H(X) − H(X|Y) = H(Y) − H(Y|X) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y).

KL in one line

Average extra bits from coding data from p with a code built for q. Same event space; not symmetric.

Bigram MLE

C(wₙ₋₁ wₙ) / C(wₙ₋₁).

Add-1 bigram

(C(h,w) + 1) / (C(h) + |V|).

Sam corpus token count

17 words + 5 </s> = 22 (start tag not counted).

HMM's three tables

Start π, transition P(tag|prev tag), emission P(word|tag); each row sums to 1.

Viterbi cost

O(T·q²) vs brute force qᵀ.

BIO tags for N entity types

2N + 1.

Skip-gram P(o|c)

exp(u_oᵀv_c) / Σ_w exp(u_wᵀv_c).

Negative sampling objective

log σ(u_oᵀv_c) + Σ_k log σ(−u_kᵀv_c).

GloVe fits what?

w_iᵀw̃_j + b_i + b̃_j ≈ log X_ij, weighted by f(X_ij).

Why fastText?

Character n-grams handle morphology (dog/dogs) and OOV words.

Marr's levels

Computational (what/why), algorithmic (how), implementation (physical).
