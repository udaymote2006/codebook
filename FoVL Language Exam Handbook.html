Prof. Deeksha Varshney: know why each fix exists

Her one named exam question: "There is a very famous question, which is usually asked: when do you apply sigmoid and softmax function?" Sigmoid for binary classification; softmax when you need a probability over all vocabulary words. D2 00:13:43

Her classes run half theory, half live coding. She asks online students to share screens, and anyone who doesn't is marked absent. Each method arrives as a fix for a named problem. A one-hot vector has no notion of similarity, so you need embeddings. The softmax denominator sums over 100,000 words, so you need negative sampling. Raw unigram sampling picks the and of, so you reshape it. Dog and dogs get unrelated vectors, so you need fastText. Expect MCQ/MSQ that names the problem and asks for the fix, or the reverse.

Three sentences that predict most of the paper

  1. Context reduces uncertainty, and every formula measures how much. Entropy, conditional entropy, MI, perplexity, n-grams and HMMs all formalise "the next symbol is predictable from what came before". Prof. Ranjan built every lecture from R4 to R8 on this.
  2. Every computation is done by hand first, then checked by a tool. He worked the Sam corpus by hand and then showed SRILM giving the same 0.6, 0.227 and −2.0177 R7 01:05:29. Expect the hand computation in the exam.
  3. Every modern method exists because the previous one failed at scale or at meaning. Counts are sparse, so learn vectors. Full softmax is slow, so sample negatives. Local windows miss global statistics, so use GloVe. Word-level vectors miss morphology, so use subwords.

What language is, and what it is for

window to mind · symbolic function · interactive function · form–meaning pair · concept ← percept · projected reality · underspecification
Sat 5 Sep · Tactiq live 1 h 41 m + video Lec1 · slides Introduction-Language 1–21 · Notion ✓

The definition he built

Language reflects patterns of thought and offers a window to the mind. You study it to describe its systematicity, its structure, and how its functions are realised slide 7. The question he wants you to ask is not only "how do I manipulate this string of words?" but "what kind of cognitive or representational system could produce this behaviour?" slide 8.

Two functions

Symbolic function

Language expresses itself through symbols: sub-word parts (dis- in distaste), whole words (cat, run, tomorrow) and strings of words. Each symbol pairs a form (spoken, written or signed) with a conventional meaning.

Interactive function

Getting ideas across: thought → linguistic representation → signal → linguistic representation → reconstructed thought. The listener rebuilds the idea; nothing is copied directly. slide 18

Why language cannot transmit reality

WorldA pear: shape, colour, texture, taste, smell
PerceptsDifferent brain areas perceive each property
ConceptIntegrated into one mental image: PEAR
FormThe word that convention pairs with that concept
The line to quote: "Language does not directly represent the external world. It represents our conceptualized experience or thoughts." R1 00:59:33 And: "Language doesn't transmit reality; it transmits symbols that allow us to reconstruct conceptualized reality." R1 01:17:05 The word cat is not the animal; it lets us reach the concept when no cat is present (slide 12, "projected reality").

The example he used for underspecification

"The cat jumped over the wall." Over allows a range of trajectories, and jump fixes neither the orientation nor the shape. "How much information does language actually specify, and how much does the listener have to infer?" slide 17 · R1 01:26:52 This is the root of the ambiguity problem in R3 and the motivation for probabilistic models later.

The one sentence to memorise: a word is a conventional form paired with a concept built from percepts, so language sends symbols for a projected reality, and the listener reconstructs it.

The problem that motivated it

If language is tied to thought, "can machines understand language?" becomes "can machines think?". From there it becomes "can we reverse-engineer cognitive processes like language and perception?" slide 22. To answer it you need a way to describe any information-processing system, brain or machine.

Turing test vs Total Turing test

Turing test (imitation game)

A human interrogator in one room, a human or a machine in the other. If the interrogator cannot tell which is which, the machine counts as intelligent. "A functional definition where we are not really explaining what is intelligence." R2 00:58:58

Total Turing test

Adds perception and actuation: the machine must also perceive and physically act like a human, moving from the two-room setup to the real world. R2 01:03:12

To pass, a computer needs NLP, knowledge representation, automated reasoning, machine learning, vision, motor control and the other senses slide 30. The vending machine slide asks how human communication connects to machine behaviour. A voice- or vision-driven vending machine maps perception to action.

Pirahã, the counter-example

Recursion was proposed as a prime feature that makes a language human and separates it from animal communication. Everett reported that Pirahã lacks it. Prof. Ranjan's verdict: "Nobody really understands the complete nature… majority of the languages have this property, so we will treat it as an outlier." Other tribal languages that were studied do show recursion, and Everett's analysis is itself contested. "This is still an ongoing question." R2 01:26:07 · 01:28:06

Exam favourite, his own example: an MCQ asking "which of the following options are correct about the Pirahã language?" R6 00:24:02. Know the three facts: it is claimed to lack recursion, it challenges recursion as the defining feature of human language, and the debate is open.
The one sentence to memorise: describe any intelligent system at three levels (goal, algorithm, hardware), designing top-down and validating bottom-up. The Turing test checks only conversational behaviour; the Total Turing test adds perceiving and acting.

Building blocks of an NLU system

  • Data: a text corpus, possibly multimodal or multilingual (Universal Dependencies), sometimes collected or annotated by crowdsourcing.
  • Processing: symbolic grammars (e.g. the English Resource Grammar) or stochastic/ML models.
  • Validation: against human performance, using performance metrics such as BLEU and cognitive metrics, which are harder to pin down. slide 40
  • A typical setup: pick a corpus, turn a hypothesis into something measurable (e.g. "adjectives help sentiment"), build the model, validate internally and externally. slide 41

Ambiguity, the main challenge

"At last, a computer that understands you like your mother" (McDonnell-Douglas ad, 1985) has three readings: as well as your mother does; that you like your mother; as well as it understands your mother. The same slide adds three more problems:

  • Speech: it might be heard as "…your lie cured mother".
  • Word sense: mother can also mean a cask used in vinegar-making.
  • Attachment: in "Copy the local patient files to disk", is it the patients or the files that are local?

slides 42–43

The data-driven turn and its limits

  • "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth, 1957). Shannon "wasn't concerned with what a message means" but with how surprising it is: information depends on probability, not meaning. This is the bridge to R4 and to D2. slide 44
  • Limits: LMs trained only on form do not learn meaning or communicative intent (Bender & Koller, 2020). BERT is insensitive to negation and to role-based event prediction (Ettinger, 2020). Data needs split languages into high- and low-resource. slide 45
The one sentence to memorise: ELIZA matches patterns while SHRDLU represents, reasons and acts, so a fluent reply never proves understanding. NLU sits where NLP's engineering and CL's models meet.

Zipf's law, Shannon's approximations, entropy

normal vs Zipfian · f·r = const · least effort · efficiency vs robustness · monkey typing · nth-order approximation · redundancy · 2.3 bits
Fri 25 Sep · Tactiq live 1 h 35 m · no video in folder · slides InfoTheory 1–39 · Notion ✓

The problem first

Many phenomena are normally distributed: few very low values, many average ones, few very high ones (three dice, life expectancy, heights). Word frequencies are not slide 5. A few words (the, of, and, to) are extremely frequent, most are extremely rare, frequent words are short and rare words longer slide 8. Can that regularity be measured?

Zipf's law

f(r) ∝ 1/r ⇔ f · r = constant

"The frequency is inversely proportional to rank. So the multiplication of frequency and rank is constant." The pattern holds across languages, like a universal law R4 00:09:54. It even holds for letters if you sort them by frequency R4 00:35:13.

Exam favourite (slide 11): "Should the 50th most common word occur with three times the frequency of the 150th?" Yes. f₅₀ / f₁₅₀ = 150 / 50 = 3. By the same rule, the most frequent word occurs twice as often as the second (slide 10).

Why are word frequencies Zipfian?

Least effort (Zipf)

If you use a word very often, it saves time and energy if that word is short.

Efficiency

Minimises the speaker's physical and cognitive effort. Short words convey their small amount of information cheaply.

Robustness

Ensures the listener can recover the meaning despite noise. Long words carry more information and survive mishearing, at a higher processing cost.

Language balances the two slide 13. Knowing these regularities helps you design NLP systems whose output mimics human statistics.

The Zipf mystery (monkey typing): students who generated random text found Zipf-like curves too. A student put it as "distribution can emerge from random character generation… the number of possible words grows exponentially with word length". Prof. Ranjan's answer: human language is not random, since you can predict the next word from context, "but as you saw it can come out of random distribution as well, so more research is required". R4 00:14:27 – 00:18:29 Do not claim Zipf proves semantics.

Joint and conditional entropy, the chain rule, MI and KL

Sat 26 Sep · Tactiq live 1 h 27 m · no video in folder · slides InfoTheory 37–52 · Notion ✓
Trap he corrected first: "Entropy, by default, does not truly represent randomness in the sequence. It's just talking about randomness of the event under study." The die and the coin are single events with no history. "Language depends on the history, so this notion of entropy will not be useful for language." You need conditional entropy. R5 00:14:32 – 00:16:56 When he asked the class what entropy says about a sequence, the answer he accepted was "it doesn't say anything about the sequences". R5 01:11:50

Joint entropy

H(X,Y) = − Σₓ Σᵧ p(x,y) log p(x,y)

The information needed on average to specify both values slide 40.

Conditional entropy: the derivation he said to know

H(Y|X) is how much extra information you still need, on average, to communicate Y when you already know X slide 41. "This is the derivation that I want you guys to really understand." R5 00:28:12

H(Y|X) = Σₓ p(x) · H(Y | X = x) average over contexts = Σₓ p(x) · [ − Σᵧ p(y|x) log p(y|x) ] expand each H(Y|X=x) = − Σₓ,ᵧ p(x) p(y|x) log p(y|x) take the minus out = − Σₓ,ᵧ p(x,y) log p(y|x) since p(x)p(y|x) = p(x,y)

The slide's umbrella example: X = weather ∈ {Sunny, Rainy}, Y = whether I carry an umbrella. Without the weather you face H(Y). Told "it's raining", you switch from p(y) to p(y|x). So H(Y|X) = p(x₁) H(Y|X=x₁) + p(x₂) H(Y|X=x₂) slides 42–43. "Language modeling is fundamentally about conditional probability and therefore conditional uncertainty" slide 41.

The chain rule

H(X,Y) = −E[log p(x,y)] = −E[log p(x) p(y|x)] = −E[log p(x)] − E[log p(y|x)] = H(X) + H(Y|X) H(X₁,…,Xₙ) = H(X₁) + H(X₂|X₁) + … + H(Xₙ|X₁,…,Xₙ₋₁)

"Multiplication gets converted into summation… then you just separate the expectations." R5 00:34:44 The information to specify X and Y is the information to specify X, plus what you still need for Y once X is known.

Student question, his answer: Abhishek Kumar asked why total joint uncertainty doesn't depend on which variable you describe first. Answer: think of the joint as a "mental bag" in which order does not exist. "It's you who brings the order", because you can say only one thing at a time. Whichever you say first, the joint is the same. R5 01:21:54 – 01:25:17

Mutual information and why it is symmetric

I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y) = H(Y) − H(Y|X) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y) I(X;Y) = Σₓ,ᵧ p(x,y) log [ p(x,y) / (p(x) p(y)) ]

MI is "the reduction of uncertainty of one RV due to knowing about the other"; in the Venn diagram it is "literally the part of uncertainty that the two variables share" slides 45–47.

Exam favourite, asked directly (slide 51 Q2): write I(X;Y) in terms of p(x), p(y) and prove I(X;Y) = I(Y;X). R5 01:11:52
Proof 1: in the sum form, swapping the roles of X and Y leaves p(x,y) unchanged (same joint table) and leaves the product p(x)p(y) unchanged, so every term is the same.
Proof 2: by the chain rule, H(X) − H(X|Y) = H(X) − [H(X,Y) − H(Y)] = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y), which is symmetric in X and Y.
Corollary: if X and Y are independent, p(x,y) = p(x)p(y), so every log is log 1 = 0 and I = 0.
He also said to practise by hand: "your mind will say that you know, but when you actually use your hand you will see the real challenge." R5 01:13:23

Examples on the slides:

  • Studying and passing: if they are unrelated, I ≈ 0; if almost every pass studied, I > 0.
  • "New Delhi": P(Delhi | New) > P(Delhi), so the two words share information. That is how MI finds collocations slides 48–49 · R5 01:04:03.

Relative entropy (KL divergence)

D(p ‖ q) = Σₓ p(x) log [ p(x) / q(x) ] = E_p [ log p(X)/q(X) ]

Two conditions from the slide and class:

  • The two distributions must be over the same event space, "else it will be apples and oranges". slide 50 · R5 01:06:37
  • The meaning: "the average number of bits that are wasted by encoding events from a distribution p with a code based on a not-quite-right distribution q".

His example: a distribution fitted on one population (he said "Neon", unclear in the transcript) may not fit India, and using it costs extra bits R5 01:07:49.

The one sentence to memorise: conditional entropy is the average leftover uncertainty after seeing the context. The chain rule adds it to H(X), MI is what the context removed (and it is symmetric), and KL is the price of coding with the wrong distribution.

From entropy to a language model

"A language model is nothing but a probability distribution over language." Training assumes your corpus is representative of English, "good enough", and estimates probabilities from it R6 00:33:50. His running example: in "peanut butter and ___", the next word Y is jelly or bread and the history X is "peanut butter and" R6 00:27:06.

Pointwise conditional entropy (surprisal): H(w|h) = − log₂ m(w | h) Total surprisal: H_total = − Σⱼ log₂ m(wⱼ | w₁…wⱼ₋₁) = − log₂ m(w₁,…,wₙ) (chain rule) Average: Ĥ = H_total / N Perplexity: PP(w) = 2^Ĥ = m(w₁,…,wₙ)^(−1/N) = ᴺ√( 1 / m(w₁,…,wₙ) )

Here m is the language model, w the next word and h the history so far slides 53–54. Log base 2 gives bits R6 00:35:33.

His definition of perplexity: "the number of possible next words that can follow any word". "Lower the perplexity, the better the language model, and in a way the model is making confident predictions." R6 00:43:14 – 00:46:07
Trap: entropy and perplexity are related (PP = 2^H) but "capturing different essence". Entropy is the expected surprise, the minimum bits to transmit. Perplexity is "the number of options that you may have per word". R6 00:47:20 – 00:47:55

Predicting sequences

"He put the coffee …": an LM assigns a probability to each possible next word. "I will be back soonish" is likelier than "I will be bassoon dish", which matters in speech recognition slide 56.

Markov assumption: P(wₙ | w₁…wₙ₋₁) ≈ P(wₙ | wₙ₋ₖ…wₙ₋₁) (recent, finite history) Unigram: P(S) = P(He) P(put) P(the) P(coffee) P(on) P(the) P(table) Bigram: P(S) = P(put|He) P(the|put) P(coffee|the) P(on|coffee) P(the|on) P(table|the) P(wₙ | wₙ₋₁) = C(wₙ₋₁ wₙ) / C(wₙ₋₁) Trigram: P(coffee | put the) = C(put the coffee) / C(put the) ← "grep the corpus"

The bigram estimate counts the pair and normalises "by the sum of all bigrams that share the same first word", which equals the unigram count of wₙ₋₁ slide 59 · R6 01:12:44. An n-gram model is a Markov model, or Markov chain.

The derivation on her slides goes in four steps, from F(wᵢ, wⱼ, w̃ₖ) = Pᵢₖ / Pⱼₖ to the loss on screen · slides 39–41:

  1. Only the vector difference matters: F(wᵢ − wⱼ, w̃ₖ), "since meaning relationships are differences in embedding space (like king − man + woman)".
  2. The relationship should be linear in the simplest way, and the output a single scalar, so use the dot product (wᵢ − wⱼ)ᵀ w̃ₖ.
  3. Make it positive by wrapping it in exp. Taking logs gives log Pᵢₖ = wᵢᵀ w̃ₖ + bᵢ + b̃ₖ.
  4. So GloVe trains wᵢ · w̃ⱼ ≈ log Xᵢⱼ.
J = Σ_{i,j} f(Xᵢⱼ) · ( wᵢᵀ w̃ⱼ + bᵢ + b̃ⱼ − log Xᵢⱼ )²

Her slide's nine implementation steps on screen · slides 37–40:

  1. Collect a corpus.
  2. Decide a context window (e.g. ±5 words).
  3. Count co-occurrences into X, where X[i,j] = how many times word j appears in the context of word i.
  4. Use the log of the counts, because absolute counts are noisy and biased by word frequency.
  5. Give each word two vectors plus biases: prediction_ij = wᵢ · w̃ⱼ + bᵢ + b̃ⱼ.
  6. Make the prediction match log X[i,j].
  7. Weight the pairs with f: about 0 when X = 0 (ignore non-occurrences), growing for small counts, saturating for very large ones, so frequent pairs do not dominate.
  8. Minimise the weighted squared error by gradient descent.
  9. Use wᵢ, or (wᵢ + w̃ᵢ)/2, as the final embedding.
The one sentence to memorise: negative sampling makes skip-gram cheap by classifying one true pair against k sampled noise pairs (drawn from U(w)^¾). GloVe instead fits dot products to log co-occurrence counts because co-occurrence ratios carry meaning, not because it is faster.

Why RNNs

The same weights at every step, so any input length works; the hidden state summarises the past.

Teacher forcing

In training, feed the gold previous token. At inference you must feed your own prediction (autoregressive). The mismatch is exposure bias.

Weaknesses

Slow on long sequences, hard to reach information many steps back, gradient problems (mentioned, not derived).

Worked in class, BIO output size: with 5 entity types, B and I for each give 10, "plus 1 for the outside tag, so output labels will be 11". With hidden size 50, the output matrix is 50 × 11. Language, levels and understanding (R1–R3)

Why can't language transmit reality?

It transmits symbols, form–meaning pairs linked to concepts built from percepts. The listener reconstructs a conceptualised, projected reality (R1 01:17:05).

"The cat jumped over the wall." What does this sentence fail to specify, and why does that matter for NLU?

The trajectory and shape of the jump. Over allows many paths. Language underspecifies and listeners infer, so a system must resolve this from context.

Distinguish the symbolic and interactive functions of language.slide

Symbolic: encoding concepts as conventional form–meaning pairs. Interactive: communicating, thought → representation → signal → representation → reconstructed thought.

You are asked to design a robot that flies. Where do you start?asked

At the computational level: what is the goal and why. Then the algorithm (flap → thrust → lift, balance). Then the implementation (wings, motors, sensors). Validate bottom-up. Jumping to hardware is the error he corrected (R2 00:44:01).

A question asks "how" a system turns input into output. Which Marr level is that?

Algorithmic (representation and transformation). "Why/what" is computational; "with what" is implementation.

A chatbot gives perfectly appropriate replies to every message. Does it understand language?

Not necessarily. ELIZA produced appropriate-looking replies by pattern matching. Understanding needs representation, reasoning and action (SHRDLU), or Total Turing abilities.

Why is the Turing test called a functional or operational definition of intelligence?

It never defines thinking; it only says that if you cannot tell the machine from a human, call it intelligent (R2 00:58:58).

What does the Total Turing test add, and why?

Perception and actuation, interacting with the real world. Human intelligence is embodied, not just conversational.

Which statements about Pirahã are correct?asked

It is claimed (by Everett) to lack recursion; that challenges recursion as the defining feature of human language; the debate is open and it is treated as an outlier (R2 01:26:07; R6 00:24:02).

Classify: a sentiment-analysis product, a computational model of human sentence processing, a system that meaningfully understands instructions.

NLP (language → information), CL (language → model), NLU (shares both goals).

"Copy the local patient files to disk." What kind of ambiguity?

Structural (attachment): is it the patients or the files that are local?

Why did Shannon's view push NLP toward data?

Information depends on probability, not meaning, and co-occurrence is information (Firth), so language can be treated empirically and computationally (slide 44).

Zipf, entropy and information (R4–R5)

Should the 50th most common word occur three times as often as the 150th?slide

Yes. f·r is constant, so the ratio is 150/50 = 3.

Random monkey-typed text also shows a Zipf-like curve. What do you conclude?

Zipf alone does not prove linguistic structure; it can arise from combinatorics. Human language differs in being predictable from context. The question is still open (R4 00:18:29).

Why are the most frequent words short?

Least effort (Zipf), and the efficiency side of the efficiency–robustness trade-off. Long words carry more information and survive noise.

Why does English need about 2.3 bits per letter, not 5?

Redundancy: letters follow strict statistical patterns, so about half the raw capacity is predictable.

What happens to Shannon-style generation as you go to 7- or 8-grams?

It "fumbles": long sequences are rarely observed, so the estimates are sparse (R4 00:49:26).

Compute the entropy of an 8-sided die and interpret it.slide

3 bits, the average message length needed to transmit an outcome (encode 1–8 as 3-bit codes).

Explain entropy and what it says about a given symbol sequence.asked

It is the expected surprise of a random variable's outcomes. On its own it says nothing about the sequence or its order; for language you need conditional entropy (R5 01:10:44, 01:11:50).

Derive H(Y|X) = −Σ p(x,y) log p(y|x).asked

H(Y|X) = Σₓ p(x) H(Y|X=x) = −Σₓ p(x) Σᵧ p(y|x) log p(y|x) = −Σ p(x,y) log p(y|x), using p(x)p(y|x) = p(x,y).

Derive the chain rule H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X).asked

−E log p(x,y) = −E log[p(x)p(y|x)] = −E log p(x) − E log p(y|x). Split the expectation.

Prove I(X;Y) = I(Y;X).asked

In Σ p(x,y) log [p(x,y)/(p(x)p(y))], both the joint and the product are unchanged by swapping X and Y. Alternatively, I = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y) is visibly symmetric.

Why does total joint uncertainty not depend on which variable you describe first?student Q

The joint is an order-free "mental bag"; you impose order only because you must say one thing at a time (R5 01:23:21).

X and Y are independent. What are I(X;Y) and H(X,Y)?

I = 0, and H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y).

How would you find collocations like "New Delhi" in a corpus?

Mutual information: P(Delhi | New) ≫ P(Delhi) means the words share information.

You fit a word distribution on one country's data and use it to code another's. What measures the cost?

KL divergence D(p‖q): the extra bits wasted coding p with q. The distributions must be over the same event space.

Tagging and syntax (R8)

Given π, transition and emission tables, find the best tags for "killer clown".asked

Score all 2² paths; AA, AN and NA are 0 because of zero emissions or transitions; NN = 0.75·0.3·0.5·0.4 = 0.045 (R8 00:26:14).

Why can't you brute-force tag a 20-word sentence with 45 tags?

45²⁰ paths is exponential. Viterbi needs about 20·45² steps.

Explain Viterbi without equations.asked

Keep the best path into each state at each step and extend only those; you never restart the search. That prunes the space drastically (R8 00:24:09).

An emission table row for tag A sums to 0.9. What's wrong?

It is not a valid distribution; emissions from a state must sum to 1 (as must outgoing transitions).

Which handles ambiguous words better, an HMM or a perceptron tagger, and why?

Typically the discriminative perceptron: it models P(T|W) with rich context over the whole sentence (R8 01:03:41).

Moore type: outputs (emissions) are attached to states.

"bb" ends in q1, which is not final. For "bac" no transition is defined for c from the current state.

Attachment ambiguity: score each structure's rules probabilistically and take the higher (R8 00:49:30).

A head → dependent relation with a functional label (subj, obj, det…), directly between words.

Vectors and embeddings (D1–D4)

The loss curve oscillates and moves away from the minimum. What do you change?

Lower the learning rate; it is overshooting.

Why stack nonlinear layers rather than linear ones?

Stacked linear layers stay a line (y = mx + c); a nonlinearity lets the model bend to the data.

When do you use sigmoid and when softmax?asked

Sigmoid for binary classification; softmax for a distribution over many classes, e.g. the next token over |V| (D2 00:13:43).

Why does word2vec keep two vectors per word?

A word plays two roles, center (v) and context (u). It does not have two meanings (D2 00:10:44).

Which word is the input in skip-gram, and how often is each word used as input?

The center word; every word becomes the center once as the window slides (D2 00:25:30).

Skip-gram training on a 100k vocabulary is too slow. Why, and what fixes it?

The softmax denominator sums over all 100k words. Negative sampling: σ on the true pair plus k sampled noise pairs.

Your negative samples are almost all "the", "of", "a". What went wrong?

You sampled from raw U(w). Use U(w)^¾ and remove stopwords in pre-processing.

What does the minus sign in log σ(−u_kᵀ v_c) do?

It pushes the center–noise dot product down, moving unrelated words apart. It is not a negative probability.

A student says GloVe exists to be cheaper than word2vec. Correct them.

GloVe exists because co-occurrence ratios carry meaning; computational cost is not the reason (D3 00:45:01).

P(water|ice)/P(water|steam) = 1.36. Does "water" help separate ice from steam?

Barely. A ratio ≈ 1 means it does not distinguish them; solid (8.9) and gas (0.085) do.

Why does GloVe weight pairs with f(X_ij)?

Rare pairs are noisy and very frequent ones shouldn't dominate. f is about 0 at X = 0, grows, then saturates.

Raw co-occurrence vectors make your classifier brittle. Why, and what do you do?

They are huge and sparse. Reduce to 25–1000 dense dimensions.

You must choose between frozen pre-trained and fine-tuned embeddings for a small sentiment dataset.

Frozen reduces overfitting but may miss task meaning; fine-tuned adapts but may forget. Combining both costs more. Decide experimentally; no one size fits all.

How do you evaluate embeddings for sentiment vs for translation?

Extrinsically: F1 for sentiment, BLEU for translation (D4 00:02:19).

"dogs" gets a vector unrelated to "dog", and "unknown" has no vector at all. One fix for both?

fastText character n-grams: compose vectors from subword pieces.

Your embedding puts "whale" next to "fish". Is the model wrong?

It is doing what it was trained to do: words sharing contexts (ocean) end up close. Embeddings encode distribution, not facts.
