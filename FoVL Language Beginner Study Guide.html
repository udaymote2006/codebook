🧒 Start From Zero

  • The problem in one sentence: before we build machines that "understand" language, we need to say what language is and what "understanding" would even mean.

  • Analogy: a recipe is not the cake. The word cat is not the animal. It is a label that lets your listener rebuild the idea of a cat in their own head.

  • What you need to know first: nothing. This block is conceptual. It sets up why later lectures use probability.

📝 Core Concepts (Crisp)

1. Language as a window to the mind (R1)

  • Definition: language reflects patterns of thought. You study it to describe its systematicity, structure, and how its functions are realised. slide 7

  • The question to ask: not only "how do I manipulate this string of words?" but "what kind of cognitive system could produce this behaviour?" slide 8

2. Two functions of language (R1)

  • Symbolic function: language uses symbols. Each symbol pairs a form (spoken, written or signed) with a conventional meaning. Symbols can be sub-word parts (dis- in distaste), words (cat), or strings of words.

  • Interactive function: communication. thought → linguistic representation → signal → linguistic representation → reconstructed thought. Nothing is copied directly; the listener rebuilds the idea. slide 18

3. Why language cannot transmit reality (R1)

  • Chain: World (a pear: shape, colour, taste) → Percepts (different brain areas sense each property) → Concept (one integrated mental image, PEAR) → Form (the word that convention pairs with that concept).

  • Quote to use: "Language does not directly represent the external world. It represents our conceptualized experience or thoughts." [R1 00:59:33]

  • And: "Language doesn't transmit reality; it transmits symbols that allow us to reconstruct conceptualized reality." [R1 01:17:05] This is called projected reality (slide 12).

4. Underspecification (R1)

  • "The cat jumped over the wall." Over allows many trajectories; jump does not fix orientation or shape.

  • Key question: "How much information does language actually specify, and how much does the listener have to infer?" [R1 01:26:52] · slide 17

  • Intuition: this is the root of ambiguity and the reason later lectures use probabilities.

5. Marr's three levels (tri-level hypothesis) (R2)

Level Question it answers Robot-fly answer accepted in class
Computational What is the goal, and why? Make the robot fly
Algorithmic How? What representation and what transformation? Flap wings → thrust → lift (aerodynamics, balance)
Implementation With what physical hardware? Wings, motors, sensors, actuators, light body

  • His correction: "You directly jump to the method. That's the rush that all the computer engineers make." Start at the computational level. [R2 00:44:01]

  • Workflow: design top-down (goal → algorithm → hardware), validate bottom-up. "Level three confirms level two, and then level two explains level one." [R2 00:44:01 · 00:52:27]

6. Turing test vs Total Turing test (R2)

  • Turing test (imitation game): an interrogator talks to a human and a machine in separate rooms. If they can't tell which is which, the machine counts as intelligent. "A functional definition where we are not really explaining what is intelligence." [R2 00:58:58]

  • Total Turing test: adds perception and actuation. The machine must also see and act like a human in the real world. [R2 01:03:12]

  • To pass, a machine needs NLP, knowledge representation, automated reasoning, machine learning, vision, motor control and other senses. slide 30

7. Pirahã (exam MCQ) (R2)

  • Recursion (embedding a phrase inside a phrase of the same type) was proposed as the feature that makes language human.

  • Daniel Everett reported that the Pirahã language lacks recursion.

  • Prof. Ranjan: "majority of the languages have this property, so we will treat it as an outlier." Everett's analysis is itself contested. "This is still an ongoing question." [R2 01:26:07 · 01:28:06]

  • Takeaway: "Producing an appropriate-looking response is not necessarily the same as understanding." [R3 00:29:04]

10. Building blocks of an NLU system (R3, slide 40)

  • Data: a corpus (possibly multilingual, e.g. Universal Dependencies; sometimes crowdsourced).

  • Processing: symbolic grammars (English Resource Grammar) or stochastic/ML models.

  • Validation: against humans, with performance metrics (BLEU) and cognitive metrics.

11. Ambiguity — the main challenge (R3, slides 42–43)

  • "At last, a computer that understands you like your mother" has 3 readings: (a) as well as your mother does; (b) that you like your mother; (c) as well as it understands your mother.

  • Speech ambiguity: heard as "…your lie cured mother".

  • Word sense: mother can also mean a cask used in vinegar-making.

  • Attachment: "Copy the local patient files to disk": are the patients or the files local?

12. The data-driven turn (R3, slides 44–45)

  • Firth (1957): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." This is the basis of word2vec in D2.

  • Shannon: information depends on probability, not meaning. This is the bridge to R4.

  • Limits: LMs trained only on form don't learn meaning or intent (Bender & Koller 2020). BERT is insensitive to negation (Ettinger 2020).

⚙️ Derivations (Stepwise) — the logical argument of the block

  1. Language is tied to thought, so "can machines understand language?" becomes "can machines think?" slide 22

  2. To answer that, we need a way to describe any information-processing system → Marr's three levels.

  3. A behaviour-only test (Turing) can be passed by pattern matching (ELIZA), so fluent output ≠ understanding.

  4. Language underspecifies meaning → the listener must infer → ambiguity everywhere.

  5. Ambiguity is resolved by choosing the most probable reading → we need probability and information theory (R4 onwards).

🧪 Key Equations

This block is conceptual. The only formula-like rule is Firth's distributional hypothesis, written formally later (D2):

meaning(w)≈distribution of contexts c in which w occurs \text{meaning}(w) \;\approx\; \text{distribution of contexts } c \text{ in which } w \text{ occurs}

  • Variables: w = a word; c = the words around it.

  • Assumption: words that occur in similar contexts have similar meanings.

  • When to use: to justify why counting or predicting context words (n-grams, word2vec, GloVe) captures meaning.

🧒 Start From Zero

  • The problem in one sentence: how do we measure how predictable language is?

  • Analogy: a guessing game. If I'm thinking of a number from 1 to 8 and you can only ask yes/no questions, you need exactly 3 questions (halve, halve, halve). "Number of yes/no questions needed" is what bits measure.

  • Prerequisites, in brief:

    • Random variable X: something with uncertain outcomes (a die roll, the next word).
    • Probability p(x): how likely outcome x is; all p(x) add up to 1.
    • log₂: "how many times do I halve to get here?" log₂ 8 = 3, log₂ 2 = 1, log₂ 1 = 0. Also log₂(1/p) = −log₂ p.
    • Joint p(x,y): probability that both happen. Conditional p(y|x): probability of y given that x happened. Link: p(x,y) = p(x)·p(y|x).
    • Expectation E[f(X)] = Σ p(x) f(x): a probability-weighted average.

📝 Core Concepts (Crisp)

1. Zipf's law (R4)

  • Problem: many things are normally distributed (heights, dice sums). Word frequencies are not: a few words (the, of, and) are very frequent and most words are rare. Frequent words are short. slides 5, 8

  • Law: frequency is inversely proportional to rank, so frequency × rank = constant. "The multiplication of frequency and rank is constant." [R4 00:09:54] It holds across languages and even for letters. [R4 00:35:13]

  • Why? Least effort (Zipf): use short words often. Efficiency (speaker saves effort) vs robustness (listener recovers meaning in noise). Language balances both. slide 13

  • Caution (monkey typing): random character generation also produces Zipf-like curves. His answer: human language is predictable from context, "but as you saw it can come out of random distribution as well, so more research is required." [R4 00:14:27–00:18:29] Do not claim Zipf proves meaning.

3. Entropy (R4)

  • Shannon's idea: "more choices meant more uncertainty, more information". slide 32

  • Definition: entropy is the expected surprise, the average of log(1/p). It is "the average length of the message needed to transmit an outcome efficiently". slides 33, 37

  • English: a uniform 26-letter alphabet needs about 5 bits/letter, but Shannon estimated about 2.3 bits for English. Redundancy ≈ 1 − 2.3/5 ≈ 50%.

  • Evidence of redundancy: "Ths sntnc s stll rdbl"; after therefor_ you know the e. slide 36

  • Activity (slide 34): A · SMALL · OBLONG · READING · LAMP · ON · THE · D… → the choices shrink at each word. "Context reduces uncertainty: basic intuition behind language modeling!"

4. Trap he corrected first (R5)

  • "Entropy, by default, does not truly represent randomness in the sequence. It's just talking about randomness of the event under study." A die has no history; "language depends on the history, so this notion of entropy will not be useful for language." You need conditional entropy. [R5 00:14:32–00:16:56]

  • Asked what entropy says about a sequence, the answer he accepted: "it doesn't say anything about the sequences". [R5 01:11:50]

5. Joint, conditional, MI, KL (R5)

  • Joint entropy H(X,Y): average bits to specify both values. slide 40

  • Conditional entropy H(Y|X): extra bits still needed for Y once you know X. slide 41 "Language modeling is fundamentally about conditional probability and therefore conditional uncertainty."

  • Chain rule: bits for (X and Y) = bits for X + extra bits for Y given X.

  • Mutual information I(X;Y): "the reduction of uncertainty of one RV due to knowing about the other"; in the Venn diagram, "the part of uncertainty that the two variables share". slides 45–47 Used to find collocations ("New Delhi": P(Delhi|New) ≫ P(Delhi)). slides 48–49

  • KL divergence D(p‖q): "the average number of bits that are wasted by encoding events from a distribution p with a code based on a not-quite-right distribution q". Both must be over the same event space, "else it will be apples and oranges". slide 50 · [R5 01:06:37]

  • Student Q (Abhishek Kumar): why doesn't joint uncertainty depend on which variable you describe first? Answer: the joint is a "mental bag" with no order; "it's you who brings the order". [R5 01:21:54–01:25:17]

⚙️ Derivations (Stepwise)

Derivation 1 — Conditional entropy (the one he said to really understand, [R5 00:28:12])

  1. Start from the definition: average the uncertainty of Y over each possible context x, weighted by how often that context occurs: H(Y|X) = Σₓ p(x) · H(Y | X = x)

  2. Expand each inner entropy using the entropy definition with the conditional distribution: H(Y | X = x) = − Σᵧ p(y|x) log p(y|x)

  3. Substitute: H(Y|X) = Σₓ p(x) · [ − Σᵧ p(y|x) log p(y|x) ]

  4. Pull the minus sign out and merge the sums: H(Y|X) = − Σₓ Σᵧ p(x) p(y|x) log p(y|x)

  5. Use p(x) p(y|x) = p(x,y) (definition of conditional probability): H(Y|X) = − Σₓ,ᵧ p(x,y) log p(y|x) ∎

Derivation 2 — Chain rule

  1. Joint entropy as an expectation: H(X,Y) = − E[ log p(X,Y) ]

  2. Factorise the joint: p(x,y) = p(x) · p(y|x)

  3. Log of a product = sum of logs ("multiplication gets converted into summation" [R5 00:34:44]): − E[ log p(X) + log p(Y|X) ]

  4. Linearity of expectation: − E[log p(X)] − E[log p(Y|X)]

  5. Recognise each term: = H(X) + H(Y|X) ∎

  6. General form: H(X₁,…,Xₙ) = H(X₁) + H(X₂|X₁) + … + H(Xₙ|X₁,…,Xₙ₋₁)

Derivation 3 — MI and its symmetry (exam question, [R5 01:11:52])

  1. Definition: I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y) (uncertainty in X minus what is left after seeing Y).

  2. Use the chain rule in the form H(X|Y) = H(X,Y) − H(Y).

  3. Substitute: I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X,Y) + H(Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y).

  4. Symmetry: the right-hand side does not change if you swap X and Y (H(X,Y) = H(Y,X), same joint table). So I(X;Y) = I(Y;X). ∎

  5. Probability form: write each entropy as an expectation over p(x,y): I(X;Y) = Σₓ,ᵧ p(x,y) log [ p(x,y) / (p(x) p(y)) ]

  6. Second proof of symmetry: in step 5, swapping X and Y leaves p(x,y) and the product p(x)p(y) unchanged, so every term is identical.

  7. Corollary: if X and Y are independent, p(x,y) = p(x)p(y), so every term is log 1 = 0 and I = 0.

🧪 Key Equations

Zipf's law

f(r)∝1r⇔f(r)⋅r=c f(r) \propto \frac{1}{r} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(r)\cdot r = c

  • Variables: f(r) = frequency of the word of rank r; c = a constant for the corpus.

  • Assumption: large natural corpus; an empirical law, approximate.

  • When to use: frequency ratios between ranks.

Entropy

H(X)=−∑xp(x)log2p(x)=𝔼[log21p(X)] H(X) = -\sum_{x} p(x)\,\log_2 p(x) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\log_2 \frac{1}{p(X)}\right]

  • Variables: p(x) = probability of outcome x. Units: bits (log base 2).

  • Assumption: a single random variable; no notion of order or history.

  • When to use: uncertainty of one event (die, coin, letter). Maximum log₂ M for M equally likely outcomes.

Joint entropy

H(X,Y)=−∑x∑yp(x,y)log2p(x,y) H(X,Y) = -\sum_{x}\sum_{y} p(x,y)\,\log_2 p(x,y)

  • Variables: p(x,y) = joint probability.

  • When to use: bits needed to specify both variables.

Conditional entropy

H(Y∣X)=∑xp(x)H(Y∣X=x)=−∑x,yp(x,y)log2p(y∣x) H(Y\mid X) = \sum_{x} p(x)\,H(Y\mid X=x) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\,\log_2 p(y\mid x)

  • Variables: p(y|x) = conditional probability.

  • Assumption: p(x) > 0 for the contexts summed over.

  • When to use: uncertainty left after seeing context. This is the language-modelling quantity. Always H(Y|X) ≤ H(Y).

Chain rule

H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y∣X)=H(Y)+H(X∣Y) H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y\mid X) = H(Y) + H(X\mid Y)

  • When to use: convert between joint and conditional entropies.

Mutual information

I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X∣Y)=H(X)+H(Y)−H(X,Y)=∑x,yp(x,y)log2p(x,y)p(x)p(y) I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X\mid Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\,\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}

  • Assumption: same joint distribution for both directions.

  • When to use: shared information; collocations; prove symmetry. I ≥ 0, I = 0 iff independent.

KL divergence (relative entropy)

D(p∥q)=∑xp(x)log2p(x)q(x)=𝔼p[log2p(X)q(X)] D(p\,\|\,q) = \sum_{x} p(x)\,\log_2\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \mathbb{E}_{p}\!\left[\log_2\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right]

  • Variables: p = true distribution; q = the model/code distribution.

  • Assumptions: same event space; q(x) > 0 wherever p(x) > 0 (else infinite).

  • When to use: cost (in bits) of using the wrong distribution. D ≥ 0; not symmetric.

💡 Exam Insights

  • Most common mistake: saying entropy describes a sequence. It doesn't; it describes one event's distribution.

  • Common mistake: in H(Y|X) forgetting to weight by p(x), or writing p(x,y) log p(x,y) instead of p(x,y) log p(y|x).

  • Shortcut: for M equally likely outcomes, H = log₂ M (die 3, coin 1, 26 letters 4.70).

⚙️ Derivations (Stepwise)

Derivation — from the chain rule to perplexity

  1. Chain rule (exact): m(w₁…w_N) = Π_{j=1..N} m(wⱼ | w₁…wⱼ₋₁)

  2. Take −log₂ (turns the product into a sum of surprisals): H_total = −log₂ m(w₁…w_N) = −Σⱼ log₂ m(wⱼ | w₁…wⱼ₋₁)

  3. Average per token: Ĥ = H_total / N

  4. Exponentiate to get back to "number of choices": PP = 2^Ĥ

  5. Simplify: 2^(−(1/N) log₂ m) = m^(−1/N), so PP = m(w₁…w_N)^(−1/N) = ᴺ√(1/m(w₁…w_N))

  6. Markov approximation: replace the full history with the last n−1 words to make it computable.

  7. Base does not matter: 10^(−(1/N) log₁₀ m) = m^(−1/N) too. SRILM prints log₁₀, so use PP = 10^(−logprob/N).

Derivation — the bigram MLE

  1. We want P(wₙ | wₙ₋₁).

  2. By definition: P(wₙ | wₙ₋₁) = P(wₙ₋₁ wₙ) / P(wₙ₋₁).

  3. Estimate both by counts over the same corpus: C(wₙ₋₁ wₙ)/T and C(wₙ₋₁)/T.

  4. The totals cancel: P(wₙ|wₙ₋₁) = C(wₙ₋₁ wₙ) / C(wₙ₋₁).

  5. Using Σ_w C(wₙ₋₁ w) in the denominator is the same thing, since every occurrence of wₙ₋₁ (that is not sentence-final) is followed by something.

⚙️ Derivations (Stepwise)

Derivation — why an HMM scores a tag sequence as π × b × a × b × …

  1. We want the best tags: T* = argmax_T P(T | W).

  2. Bayes: P(T|W) = P(W|T) P(T) / P(W). P(W) is the same for every T, so drop it: T* = argmax_T P(W|T) P(T) (= the joint P(W,T); this is why HMM is generative).

  3. Assumption 1 (output independence): each word depends only on its own tag: P(W|T) ≈ Π_t b_{t_t}(w_t).

  4. Assumption 2 (Markov / bigram on tags): each tag depends only on the previous tag: P(T) ≈ π(t₁) Π_{t≥2} a(t_{t−1} → t_t).

  5. Combine: P(W,T) = π(t₁) b_{t₁}(w₁) Π_{t=2..T} a(t_{t−1}→t_t) b_{t_t}(w_t).

  6. Compare with an n-gram LM, which uses P(British) P(Left|British)… directly on words. The HMM goes through hidden tags: P(N) P(British|N) · P(N|N) P(Left|N) · … slide 20

Derivation — the Viterbi recursion

  1. Define δ_t(j) = probability of the best tag path that ends in tag j at position t.

  2. Base case: δ₁(j) = π_j · b_j(w₁).

  3. Key insight: the best path into j at time t must extend the best path into some state i at t−1 (any other path into i is worse and stays worse after multiplying by the same a_ij b_j(w_t)).

  4. Recursion: δ_t(j) = max_i [δ_{t−1}(i) · a_ij] · b_j(w_t). Store the winning i in ψ_t(j).

  5. Termination: best final probability = max_j δ_T(j).

  6. Backtrace: follow ψ pointers from the best final state back to t = 1.

  7. Cost: at each of T positions, for each of q states, check q predecessors → O(T·q²) instead of O(q^T).

🔁 Algorithms / Process — Viterbi pseudocode

Input: words w[1..T], tags 1..q, start pi[j], transition a[i][j], emission b[j][word]
1. For each tag j:
       delta[1][j] = pi[j] * b[j][w[1]]
       back[1][j]  = none
2. For t = 2 to T:
       For each tag j:
           best_score = 0, best_prev = none
           For each tag i:
               score = delta[t-1][i] * a[i][j]
               if score > best_score: best_score = score, best_prev = i
           delta[t][j] = best_score * b[j][w[t]]
           back[t][j]  = best_prev
3. last = the tag j with the largest delta[T][j]
4. path[T] = last
   For t = T down to 2: path[t-1] = back[t][path[t]]
5. Return path and delta[T][last]

  • Time: O(T · q²). Memory: O(T · q).

  • Practical note (standard): multiply many small numbers → underflow; in code use log-probabilities and add instead of multiply.

📉 Convergence & Behavior

  • Exact: Viterbi always returns the true best path under the HMM (no approximation), because of the optimal-substructure argument in step 3 of the derivation.

  • Edge case: a zero emission (A cannot emit killer) kills every path through that state. In real systems, smooth emissions.

  • Edge case: ties — any argmax is valid; state the tie-break.

  • Limit: HMM assumptions (each word depends only on its tag) are weak for ambiguous words; discriminative and neural taggers do better.

Gradient descent update

θ←θ−α∂L∂θ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \,\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta}

  • In words: new parameter = old parameter − (step size) × (slope of the loss).

  • Symbols: θ = any parameter (scalar, or a whole matrix — the rule applies to every entry); α = learning rate (scalar > 0); ∂L/∂θ = partial derivative, same shape as θ.

  • Why the minus sign: the slope points uphill; we want downhill. Slope > 0 → θ decreases; slope < 0 → θ increases. Exactly her rule.

  • Several parameters: take the partial derivative w.r.t. each one, holding the others fixed.

Sigmoid

σ(z)=11+e−z,σ′(z)=σ(z)(1−σ(z)),σ(−z)=1−σ(z) \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}, \qquad \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\,\big(1-\sigma(z)\big), \qquad \sigma(-z) = 1-\sigma(z)

  • Shape: scalar in, scalar out (applied element-wise to vectors).

  • Tiny run: σ(0) = 0.5; σ(2) = 1/(1 + e⁻²) = 1/(1 + 0.135) = 0.881; σ(−2) = 0.119.

  • Use: a probability for one yes/no decision.

softmax(𝐳)k=ezk∑j=1Kezj \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}

  • Shape: vector of K scores in, vector of K probabilities out (all > 0, sum to 1).

  • Tiny run: z = [2, 1, 0] → e^z = [7.389, 2.718, 1.000], sum 11.107 → [0.665, 0.245, 0.090].

Step 1 — the probability of one context word

P(o∣c)=exp(𝐮o⊤𝐯c)∑w∈Vexp(𝐮w⊤𝐯c) P(o \mid c) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)}{\sum_{w \in V}\exp(\mathbf{u}_w^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)}

  • In words: "how likely is word o to appear near center word c" = (how similar o's context vector is to c's center vector), normalised over all words.

  • Shapes: v_c ∈ ℝ^d (column of the center matrix V_emb, size |V| × d); u_o ∈ ℝ^d (row of the context matrix U, |V| × d); uᵀv is a scalar. The denominator has |V| terms.

  • Why exp: makes every score positive, so we can normalise into probabilities.

Step 2 — the likelihood of the whole corpus, then the loss

L(θ)=∏t=1T∏−m≤j≤mj≠0P(wt+j∣wt;θ)⇒J(θ)=−1T∑t=1T∑−m≤j≤mj≠0logP(wt+j∣wt;θ) L(\theta) = \prod_{t=1}^{T}\ \prod_{\substack{-m\le j\le m\\ j\ne 0}} P(w_{t+j}\mid w_t;\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad J(\theta) = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ \sum_{\substack{-m\le j\le m\\ j\ne 0}} \log P(w_{t+j}\mid w_t;\theta)

  • In words: maximise the probability of every true (center, context) pair. Equivalently, minimise the average negative log-probability.

  • Why −log and 1/T: log turns the giant product into a sum (no underflow); minus turns "maximise" into "minimise"; 1/T makes it an average.

  • θ = all entries of both matrices = 2 × |V| × d numbers.

Step 3 — the gradient for one pair (derived)

For one pair, J = −log P(o|c) = −u_oᵀv_c + log Σ_w exp(u_wᵀv_c).

  1. Derivative of the first term w.r.t. v_c: ∂(−u_oᵀv_c)/∂v_c = −u_o.

  2. Derivative of the log-sum: chain rule → (1 / Σ_w exp(u_wᵀv_c)) · Σ_x exp(u_xᵀv_c) · u_x.

  3. Recognise the softmax: exp(u_xᵀv_c)/Σ_w exp(u_wᵀv_c) = P(x|c). So the second term is Σ_x P(x|c) u_x.

  4. Combine:

∂J∂𝐯c=−𝐮o+∑x∈VP(x∣c)𝐮x=𝔼x∼P(⋅∣c)[𝐮x]⏟what the model expects−𝐮o⏟what actually occurred \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{v}_c} = -\mathbf{u}_o + \sum_{x\in V} P(x\mid c)\,\mathbf{u}_x \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P(\cdot\mid c)}[\mathbf{u}_x]}_{\text{what the model expects}} - \underbrace{\mathbf{u}_o}_{\text{what actually occurred}}

  1. For the context vectors (same method): ∂J/∂u_x = (P(x|c) − 𝟙[x = o]) · v_c. This is the familiar "predicted − true" (ŷ − y) of softmax + cross-entropy.

  2. What the update does: v_c ← v_c − α(E[u] − u_o) moves v_c towards the true context u_o and away from the words the model wrongly expected. That is why similar-context words drift together.

  3. The cost: the sum over x ∈ V touches every word, every step. With |V| ≈ 100,000 that is slow → negative sampling (D3).

🔁 Algorithms / Process — skip-gram, in the order she required

1. Read the corpus; build the vocabulary of unique words.
2. Build word_to_id and id_to_word dictionaries.
3. Initialise two matrices of shape |V| x d with small random numbers: center vectors V, context vectors U.
4. Slide a window of size m: for every position t, for every j in -m..m (j != 0), add pair (id of w[t], id of w[t+j]). Print the pairs.
5. For each pair (c, o):
     a. v_c = row c of V
     b. scores = U times v_c            (|V| numbers)
     c. probs = softmax(scores)
     d. loss = -log(probs[o])           (cross-entropy against the one-hot target)
     e. grad_v = (U transposed times probs) - U[o]
        grad_U = outer(probs - onehot(o), v_c)
     f. V[c] = V[c] - lr * grad_v ;  U = U - lr * grad_U
6. Repeat for several epochs; use rows of V (or V + U) as the embeddings.
  • Complexity per pair: O(|V| · d) (the softmax over the whole vocabulary). Per epoch: O(T · 2m · |V| · d).

📉 Convergence & Behavior

  • Converges like any softmax classifier trained by SGD, if the learning rate is small enough.

  • Slow when |V| is large: every step touches all |V| context vectors.

  • Rare words get few updates → noisy vectors.

A. The negative-sampling loss

Jneg(𝐮o,𝐯c)=−logσ(𝐮o⊤𝐯c)−∑k=1Klogσ(−𝐮k⊤𝐯c),k∼Pn(w)∝U(w)3/4 J_{\text{neg}}(\mathbf{u}_o,\mathbf{v}_c) = -\log \sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c) \;-\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \sigma(-\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c), \qquad k \sim P_n(w) \propto U(w)^{3/4}

  • In words: make the true pair's "is-a-pair" probability σ(u_o·v_c) close to 1, and each noise pair's probability close to 0 (because σ(−x) = 1 − σ(x), maximising σ(−u_k·v_c) = minimising σ(u_k·v_c)).

  • Shapes: all vectors ∈ ℝ^d; each σ(·) is a scalar. Only K + 1 context vectors are touched (K ≈ 5–20), not |V|.

Gradient (derived) — using d/dx log σ(x) = 1 − σ(x) and d/dx log σ(−x) = −σ(x):

  1. True pair term: ∂/∂v_c [−log σ(u_o·v_c)] = −(1 − σ(u_o·v_c)) u_o = (σ(u_o·v_c) − 1) u_o.

  2. Each noise term: ∂/∂v_c [−log σ(−u_k·v_c)] = σ(u_k·v_c) u_k.

∂J∂𝐯c=(σ(𝐮o⊤𝐯c)−1)𝐮o+∑k=1Kσ(𝐮k⊤𝐯c)𝐮k,∂J∂𝐮o=(σ(𝐮o⊤𝐯c)−1)𝐯c,∂J∂𝐮k=σ(𝐮k⊤𝐯c)𝐯c \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{v}_c} = \big(\sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)-1\big)\mathbf{u}_o + \sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma(\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)\,\mathbf{u}_k, \qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{u}_o} = \big(\sigma(\mathbf{u}_o^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)-1\big)\mathbf{v}_c, \qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mathbf{u}_k} = \sigma(\mathbf{u}_k^{\top}\mathbf{v}_c)\,\mathbf{v}_c

  1. What the update does: (σ − 1) is negative, so subtracting the gradient adds a bit of u_o to v_c (pull together). σ(u_k·v_c) is positive, so subtracting removes a bit of u_k (push apart). B. The GloVe derivation (her four steps, slides 39–41)

  1. Start: we want a function of three word vectors that reproduces the ratio: F(wᵢ, wⱼ, w̃ₖ) = Pᵢₖ / Pⱼₖ.

  2. Only the difference matters: "meaning relationships are differences in embedding space (like king − man + woman)" → F(wᵢ − wⱼ, w̃ₖ).

  3. Simplest linear form, scalar output: use the dot product → F((wᵢ − wⱼ)ᵀ w̃ₖ) = Pᵢₖ / Pⱼₖ.

  4. Turn a difference into a ratio: choose F = exp, because exp(a − b) = exp(a)/exp(b). Then exp(wᵢᵀw̃ₖ) = Pᵢₖ, i.e. wᵢᵀw̃ₖ = log Pᵢₖ = log Xᵢₖ − log Xᵢ.

  5. Absorb the word-only term into biases: log Xᵢ depends only on i → becomes bᵢ; add b̃ₖ for symmetry: wᵢᵀw̃ₖ + bᵢ + b̃ₖ = log Xᵢₖ.

  6. Fit it by weighted least squares over all pairs that co-occur:

J=∑i,j=1|V|f(Xij)(𝐰i⊤𝐰̃j+bi+b̃j−logXij)2 J = \sum_{i,j=1}^{|V|} f(X_{ij})\,\big(\mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_j + b_i + \tilde b_j - \log X_{ij}\big)^2

  • Shapes: X is |V| × |V| counts; wᵢ, w̃ⱼ ∈ ℝ^d; bᵢ, b̃ⱼ scalars.

  • Why f(X) (her slide): about 0 when X = 0 (ignore non-occurrences; also log 0 is undefined), growing for small counts, saturating for large ones so frequent pairs don't dominate. Beyond the lecture: the paper's choice is f(x) = (x/x_max)^{3/4} for x < x_max (x_max = 100), else 1.

  • Why log X: "absolute counts are noisy and biased by word frequency" (slide 37).

  • Final embedding: wᵢ or (wᵢ + w̃ᵢ)/2.

🔁 Algorithms / Process — GloVe, her nine slide steps

1. Collect a corpus.
2. Decide a context window (e.g. plus/minus 5 words).
3. Count co-occurrences into X: X[i][j] = times word j appears in the context of word i.
4. Use the log of the counts (raw counts are noisy and frequency-biased).
5. Give each word two vectors w_i, w_tilde_j and two biases b_i, b_tilde_j:
   prediction_ij = dot(w_i, w_tilde_j) + b_i + b_tilde_j
6. Make prediction_ij match log X[i][j].
7. Weight each pair with f(X[i][j]): 0 when X is 0, growing for small counts, capped for large ones.
8. Minimise sum over pairs of f(X[i][j]) * (prediction_ij - log X[i][j])^2 by gradient descent.
9. Use w_i, or (w_i + w_tilde_i) / 2, as the final embedding.
  • Complexity: building X is one pass over the corpus, O(T · window). Each training epoch is O(nnz(X) · d), where nnz = number of non-zero cells (zero cells have f = 0 and are skipped).

  • GloVe: pairs with X = 0 contribute nothing (would give log 0); very frequent pairs are capped by f.

  • The problem in one sentence: a fixed window forgets anything older than n words; we want a model that reads any length and remembers.

📝 Core Concepts (Crisp)

  • Why RNNs: the same weights at every step, so any input length works; the hidden state summarises the past.

  • Teacher forcing: in training, feed the gold previous token. At inference, feed your own prediction (autoregressive). The mismatch is exposure bias.

  • Weaknesses: slow on long sequences (step by step, no parallelism), hard to use information many steps back, gradient problems (mentioned in class, not derived).

The RNN language model

𝐡t=ϕ(𝐖xh𝐱t+𝐖hh𝐡t−1+𝐛h),𝐨t=𝐖ho𝐡t+𝐛o,p(yt)=softmax(𝐨t) \mathbf{h}_t = \phi(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_h), \qquad \mathbf{o}_t = \mathbf{W}_{ho}\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_o, \qquad p(y_t) = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{o}_t)

  • In words: new memory = squash(what I see now + what I remembered); then score every vocabulary word and softmax.

  • Shapes: x_t ∈ ℝ^{d_x} (word embedding); h_t ∈ ℝ^{d_h}; W_xh: d_h × d_x; W_hh: d_h × d_h; W_ho: |V| × d_h; o_t ∈ ℝ^{|V|}. φ = tanh (or ReLU).

  • The same Ws at every t — that's "weight sharing".

Loss and parameter count

  • The loss is the sum of the per-step cross-entropies (same as skip-gram's, D2).

Why gradients vanish or explode (Beyond the lecture: she mentioned it, didn't derive it)

∂𝐡T∂𝐡1=∏t=2T∂𝐡t∂𝐡t−1=∏t=2Tdiag(ϕ′(⋅))𝐖hh \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_T}{\partial \mathbf{h}_1} = \prod_{t=2}^{T} \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}} = \prod_{t=2}^{T} \operatorname{diag}\!\big(\phi'(\cdot)\big)\,\mathbf{W}_{hh}

  • Backprop through time multiplies the same kind of factor T−1 times. If each factor is < 1 in size, the product shrinks to ~0 (vanishing: the model can't learn long-range links). If > 1, it blows up (exploding: fix with gradient clipping). LSTMs/GRUs add gates to keep this product near 1.

🔁 Algorithms / Process — autoregressive generation

1. h = zero vector; token = start symbol
2. Repeat until end symbol or max length:
     a. x = embedding(token)
     b. h = tanh(W_xh * x + W_hh * h + b_h)
     c. probs = softmax(W_ho * h + b_o)
     d. token = most likely word (greedy) or a sample from probs
     e. append token to output
3. Return the output

  • Training uses the gold previous token instead of step 2d (teacher forcing).

  • Complexity: O(T · (d_h² + d_h·d_x + d_h·|V|)) per sequence; steps are sequential (no parallelism across t).

  • Teacher forcing vs autoregressive: gold token in training vs own prediction at inference.

  • Sequence labelling = one label per token.

🧾 The one sentence to memorise

An RNN reuses one set of weights over time, so it reads any length; train it with teacher forcing and run it autoregressively; BIO needs 2N + 1 labels.

Problem 6 (slide 70): Why must the end-to-end model prune?

  1. A sentence of T words has T(T+1)/2 contiguous spans.

  2. "The fluffy cat stuck in the tree": T = 7 → 7 × 8/2 = 28 spans → 28 × 27/2 = 378 span pairs.

  3. A 1,000-word document: 1000 × 1001/2 = 500,500 spans → about 500,500²/2 ≈ 1.25 × 10¹¹ pairs.

  4. Answer: O(T²) spans, O(T⁴) pairs → keep only spans with high s_m.

🔁 Algorithms / Process — mention pair at test time

1. Detect candidate mentions m1..mN (pronouns, named entities, noun phrases).
2. For i = 2 to N:
       For j = 1 to i-1:
           p = classifier(m_j, m_i)
           if p > threshold: add edge (m_j, m_i)
3. Clusters = connected components of the edge graph (this is the transitive closure).
4. Drop singleton clusters if your convention says so.
  • Complexity: O(N²) classifier calls; closure by union-find is near O(N²) in the number of edges. Mention ranking is also O(N²) scores but keeps ≤ 1 edge per mention. End-to-end: O(T²) spans before pruning.

📉 Convergence & Behavior

  • Mention pair: unstable under transitivity — one false positive merges clusters; one false negative splits them.

  • Mention ranking: more stable (≤ 1 link per mention) but still uses closure.

  • Clustering: uses whole-cluster evidence, so it is more robust to one misleading pair.

  • End-to-end: no detection errors to propagate, but the cost forces aggressive pruning.

💡 Exam Insights

  • Most likely question: Q6 style — chains + instances + describe both models (numerical).

  • Pattern: each model fixes the last: pairs explode → rank; pipeline errors → end-to-end; lone pairs ambiguous → cluster.

  • Confusions she addressed: negative sampling (D3) ≠ negative instances (D6); parser head ≠ attention span head; anaphora ≠ coreference (bridging).

🧾 The one sentence to memorise

Coreference is detect-then-cluster: mention pair classifies every pair and takes the transitive closure (so one bad link explodes a chain), mention ranking gives each mention one antecedent or NA, the end-to-end model scores every span with s(i, j) = s_m(i) + s_m(j) + s_a(i, j), and clustering merges whole clusters because clusters carry more signal than pairs.

Language, levels and understanding (R1–R3)

Why can't language transmit reality?

It transmits symbols, form–meaning pairs linked to concepts built from percepts. The listener reconstructs a conceptualised, projected reality (R1 01:17:05).

"The cat jumped over the wall." What does this sentence fail to specify, and why does that matter for NLU?

The trajectory and shape of the jump. Over allows many paths. Language underspecifies and listeners infer, so a system must resolve this from context.

Distinguish the symbolic and interactive functions of language.slide

Symbolic: encoding concepts as conventional form–meaning pairs. Interactive: communicating, thought → representation → signal → representation → reconstructed thought.

You are asked to design a robot that flies. Where do you start?asked

At the computational level: what is the goal and why. Then the algorithm (flap → thrust → lift, balance). Then the implementation (wings, motors, sensors). Validate bottom-up. Jumping to hardware is the error he corrected (R2 00:44:01).

A question asks "how" a system turns input into output. Which Marr level is that?

Algorithmic (representation and transformation). "Why/what" is computational; "with what" is implementation.

A chatbot gives perfectly appropriate replies to every message. Does it understand language?

Not necessarily. ELIZA produced appropriate-looking replies by pattern matching. Understanding needs representation, reasoning and action (SHRDLU), or Total Turing abilities.

Why is the Turing test called a functional or operational definition of intelligence?

It never defines thinking; it only says that if you cannot tell the machine from a human, call it intelligent (R2 00:58:58).

What does the Total Turing test add, and why?

Perception and actuation, interacting with the real world. Human intelligence is embodied, not just conversational.

Which statements about Pirahã are correct?asked

It is claimed (by Everett) to lack recursion; that challenges recursion as the defining feature of human language; the debate is open and it is treated as an outlier (R2 01:26:07; R6 00:24:02).

Classify: a sentiment-analysis product, a computational model of human sentence processing, a system that meaningfully understands instructions.

NLP (language → information), CL (language → model), NLU (shares both goals).

"Copy the local patient files to disk." What kind of ambiguity?

Structural (attachment): is it the patients or the files that are local?

Why did Shannon's view push NLP toward data?

Information depends on probability, not meaning, and co-occurrence is information (Firth), so language can be treated empirically and computationally (slide 44).

Zipf, entropy and information (R4–R5)

Should the 50th most common word occur three times as often as the 150th?slide

Yes. f·r is constant, so the ratio is 150/50 = 3.

Random monkey-typed text also shows a Zipf-like curve. What do you conclude?

Zipf alone does not prove linguistic structure; it can arise from combinatorics. Human language differs in being predictable from context. The question is still open (R4 00:18:29).

Why are the most frequent words short?

Least effort (Zipf), and the efficiency side of the efficiency–robustness trade-off. Long words carry more information and survive noise.

Why does English need about 2.3 bits per letter, not 5?

Redundancy: letters follow strict statistical patterns, so about half the raw capacity is predictable.

What happens to Shannon-style generation as you go to 7- or 8-grams?

It "fumbles": long sequences are rarely observed, so the estimates are sparse (R4 00:49:26).

Compute the entropy of an 8-sided die and interpret it.slide

3 bits, the average message length needed to transmit an outcome (encode 1–8 as 3-bit codes).

Explain entropy and what it says about a given symbol sequence.asked

It is the expected surprise of a random variable's outcomes. On its own it says nothing about the sequence or its order; for language you need conditional entropy (R5 01:10:44, 01:11:50).

Derive H(Y|X) = −Σ p(x,y) log p(y|x).asked

H(Y|X) = Σₓ p(x) H(Y|X=x) = −Σₓ p(x) Σᵧ p(y|x) log p(y|x) = −Σ p(x,y) log p(y|x), using p(x)p(y|x) = p(x,y).

Derive the chain rule H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y|X).asked

−E log p(x,y) = −E log[p(x)p(y|x)] = −E log p(x) − E log p(y|x). Split the expectation.

Prove I(X;Y) = I(Y;X).asked

In Σ p(x,y) log [p(x,y)/(p(x)p(y))], both the joint and the product are unchanged by swapping X and Y. Alternatively, I = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y) is visibly symmetric.

Why does total joint uncertainty not depend on which variable you describe first?student Q

The joint is an order-free "mental bag"; you impose order only because you must say one thing at a time (R5 01:23:21).

X and Y are independent. What are I(X;Y) and H(X,Y)?

I = 0, and H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y).

How would you find collocations like "New Delhi" in a corpus?

Mutual information: P(Delhi | New) ≫ P(Delhi) means the words share information.

You fit a word distribution on one country's data and use it to code another's. What measures the cost?

KL divergence D(p‖q): the extra bits wasted coding p with q. The distributions must be over the same event space.

Perplexity, n-grams and smoothing (R6–R7)

Define perplexity the way Prof. Ranjan did.

2^Ĥ, the number of possible next words that can follow any word. Lower means a more confident, better LM.

LM A reports perplexity 120, LM B reports 95 with a different vocabulary. Which is better?asked

You can't say. Perplexities are comparable only with identical vocabularies (R7 00:43:08).

A teammate tuned λ on the test set and reports a lower perplexity.

Invalid: the model then has knowledge of the test set. Tune on dev, report once on test.

From the Markov chain diagram, what is P("he is")?asked

0.6 from the He → is arc (accepted in class). With π(He) = 0.7, the full probability is 0.42.

Can a weighted FSA for language have a self-loop?student Q

Yes. Repetition happens ("he, he is my brother"), per Prof. Ranjan's answer to the objection (R6 01:25:20).

How many tokens do you divide by for unigrams in the 5-sentence Sam corpus?asked

22: 17 words + 5 </s>. Not 27; <s> is redundant (R7 00:03:58).

Under a bigram model, which is likelier: "Sam I do I like" or "Sam I am"?slide

"Sam I am": 0.072 vs 0.0096.

Two sentences differ 30× in unigram probability but have almost equal ppl. How?

Perplexity normalises by length, which compensates for the shorter sentence's fewer factors (R6–R7 Problem 3).

A test sentence contains a word never seen in training. What happens, and what fixes it?

The MLE probability is 0, so P(S) = 0 and PP = ∞. Map it to UNK and smooth (Laplace gives 1/29 on the toy data).

A trigram is unseen but its bigram is frequent. Should you back off automatically?

No. It may be a "grammatical null", an implausible sequence. Prefer interpolation with tuned λ (R7 00:50:59).

Why take logs of likelihood?

Products of many small probabilities underflow; logs turn the product into a stable sum.

What does SRILM use if you give no smoothing flag?

Good-Turing discounting. -addsmooth 0 gives unsmoothed; -addsmooth 1 gives Laplace.

What is the difference between ppl and ppl1 in SRILM output?

ppl divides by words + sentences (counts </s>); ppl1 divides by words only.

Unigram, bigram and trigram on WSJ give perplexity 962, 170 and 109. What does that show, and what's the catch?

More context gives lower perplexity, but higher orders get sparser, so you need smoothing or interpolation.

Tagging and syntax (R8)

Given π, transition and emission tables, find the best tags for "killer clown".asked

Score all 2² paths; AA, AN and NA are 0 because of zero emissions or transitions; NN = 0.75·0.3·0.5·0.4 = 0.045 (R8 00:26:14).

Why can't you brute-force tag a 20-word sentence with 45 tags?

45²⁰ paths is exponential. Viterbi needs about 20·45² steps.

Explain Viterbi without equations.asked

Keep the best path into each state at each step and extend only those; you never restart the search. That prunes the space drastically (R8 00:24:09).

An emission table row for tag A sums to 0.9. What's wrong?

It is not a valid distribution; emissions from a state must sum to 1 (as must outgoing transitions).

Which handles ambiguous words better, an HMM or a perceptron tagger, and why?

Typically the discriminative perceptron: it models P(T|W) with rich context over the whole sentence (R8 01:03:41).

Is an HMM a Mealy-type or a Moore-type machine?

Moore type: outputs (emissions) are attached to states.

FSA with F = {q3}: why is "bb" rejected and "bac" not even processed?

"bb" ends in q1, which is not final. For "bac" no transition is defined for c from the current state.

Tag "We saw the yellow dog" with IOB chunks.

We/B-NP saw/B-VP the/B-NP yellow/I-NP dog/I-NP.

How do you test whether a group of words is a constituent?

See whether it moves as a unit: "[to the children] [about drugs]" can swap; splitting them is ungrammatical.

"I shot an elephant in my pajamas" has two parses. How do you pick one?

Attachment ambiguity: score each structure's rules probabilistically and take the higher (R8 00:49:30).

What does a dependency arc encode that a phrase-structure node does not?

A head → dependent relation with a functional label (subj, obj, det…), directly between words.

Vectors and embeddings (D1–D4)

The loss curve oscillates and moves away from the minimum. What do you change?

Lower the learning rate; it is overshooting.

Why stack nonlinear layers rather than linear ones?

Stacked linear layers stay a line (y = mx + c); a nonlinearity lets the model bend to the data.

When do you use sigmoid and when softmax?asked

Sigmoid for binary classification; softmax for a distribution over many classes, e.g. the next token over |V| (D2 00:13:43).

Why does word2vec keep two vectors per word?

A word plays two roles, center (v) and context (u). It does not have two meanings (D2 00:10:44).

Which word is the input in skip-gram, and how often is each word used as input?

The center word; every word becomes the center once as the window slides (D2 00:25:30).

Skip-gram training on a 100k vocabulary is too slow. Why, and what fixes it?

The softmax denominator sums over all 100k words. Negative sampling: σ on the true pair plus k sampled noise pairs.

Your negative samples are almost all "the", "of", "a". What went wrong?

You sampled from raw U(w). Use U(w)^¾ and remove stopwords in pre-processing.

What does the minus sign in log σ(−u_kᵀ v_c) do?

It pushes the center–noise dot product down, moving unrelated words apart. It is not a negative probability.

A student says GloVe exists to be cheaper than word2vec. Correct them.

GloVe exists because co-occurrence ratios carry meaning; computational cost is not the reason (D3 00:45:01).

P(water|ice)/P(water|steam) = 1.36. Does "water" help separate ice from steam?

Barely. A ratio ≈ 1 means it does not distinguish them; solid (8.9) and gas (0.085) do.

Why does GloVe weight pairs with f(X_ij)?

Rare pairs are noisy and very frequent ones shouldn't dominate. f is about 0 at X = 0, grows, then saturates.

Raw co-occurrence vectors make your classifier brittle. Why, and what do you do?

They are huge and sparse. Reduce to 25–1000 dense dimensions.

You must choose between frozen pre-trained and fine-tuned embeddings for a small sentiment dataset.

Frozen reduces overfitting but may miss task meaning; fine-tuned adapts but may forget. Combining both costs more. Decide experimentally; no one size fits all.

How do you evaluate embeddings for sentiment vs for translation?

Extrinsically: F1 for sentiment, BLEU for translation (D4 00:02:19).

"dogs" gets a vector unrelated to "dog", and "unknown" has no vector at all. One fix for both?

fastText character n-grams: compose vectors from subword pieces.

Your embedding puts "whale" next to "fish". Is the model wrong?

It is doing what it was trained to do: words sharing contexts (ocean) end up close. Embeddings encode distribution, not facts.

Coreference (D6)

Define coreference resolution and name its two steps.

Identify all mentions that refer to the same real-world entity. Step 1: detect the mentions (pronouns, named entities, noun phrases), which is relatively easy. Step 2: cluster them by entity, which is hard (slide 9).

"She poured water from the pitcher into the cup until it was full." Change full to empty. Why does this break coreference systems?

The referent of it flips (cup → pitcher) on world knowledge, not grammar. These are Winograd schemas; LLMs still miss them, and for some sentences humans disagree because it is "very subjective" (D6 00:05:39).

Are "It" in "It is sunny", "No student" and "100 miles" mentions?slide

This is a gray area. Marking all pronouns, NEs and NPs over-generates mentions, so pick a convention and go with it (slides 12–13).

Your mention detector marks every NP and the output is noisy. Give two fixes.

Train a classifier to filter spurious mentions, or (much more common) keep all as candidates and discard singletons after coreference runs (slide 14). She restricted her own system to pronouns and NEs because adding NPs "can create noise" (D6 00:14:40).

"We went to see a concert last night. The tickets were really expensive." Is this coreference?

No. It is bridging anaphora: the tickets depends on a concert for its interpretation but is not the same entity (slide 18).

You are building coreference for a corpus of short stories. Which reference type should your design prioritise, and why?

Cataphora (forward reference, "Before she arrived, Anna…"), which is common in storytelling. "If you know that property beforehand, only you will be able to make a more robust model" (D6 00:20:07).

Why are "my" and "she" in one cluster in "I voted for Nader… my values," she said?student Q

Her first answer was English grammar: the quoted speaker I is she (direct speech). Later she conceded the sentence is ambiguous: two clusters or three are both defensible, and "the ground truth… can also be ambiguous" (D6 00:10:56 · 00:22:30).

Are mention-pair negative examples the same thing as word2vec's negative sampling?student Q

No. Negative sampling was to avoid the full-vocabulary softmax ("to reduce the vocabulary and the size issue"). Here negatives exist "to prepare the data for training" a binary classifier (D6 00:26:36).

How do you build training data for a mention-pair classifier?

From annotated clusters: pairs of coreferent mentions are positives (label 1), other pairs are negatives. Keep the set balanced. "If you want to make the model understand that she and my is the correct pair, you also need… she and he is not" (D6 00:25:17).

Scores: (I,my) 0.98, (Nader,he) 0.95, (she,I) 0.85, the rest < 0.1. Threshold 0.5. What are the clusters?

Links I–my, Nader–he and she–I. Closure gives {I, my, she} and {Nader, he} (D6 Problem 2).

A mention-pair system wrongly scores p(Nader, I) = 0.7. What happens, and what is this failure called?

After transitive closure every mention merges into one giant cluster ("chain explosion"). One missed link instead gives "broken clusters". In class: "transitivity may lead to error propagation" (D6 00:38:24).

Why move from mention pair to mention ranking?

Many mentions have only one clear antecedent, but mention pair asks the model to predict all of them, including far ones that are "almost impossible". Ranking picks one best antecedent, which is more linguistically plausible (slide 34).

A mention refers to nothing earlier in the text. How does mention ranking handle it?

A dummy NA candidate: if NA scores highest, no link is added. "There will be many words which are not in the co-reference", so training data needs those cases (D6 00:41:11).

"John bought him a new car." Which feature family rules out John as the antecedent of "him"?

Syntactic constraints (slide 46). Others: person/number/gender agreement, semantic compatibility, recency, grammatical role, parallelism.

What goes into the input layer of the neural coref model?

Candidate-antecedent embeddings and features, mention embeddings and features, and additional features (distance, genre, speaker). Embed the head word, first and last words and neighbours rather than the whole mention (slides 48–49; D6 00:53:18).

Why did deep learning replace feature-based coreference?asked

She asked "Why deep learning came into picture after machine learning?" and answered "Better features": learned rather than hand-built (D6 01:07:59 · 01:08:23).

How does the end-to-end model do without a mention-detection step?

It treats every span (up to a length) as a candidate. It builds gᵢ = [x*START, x*END, x̂ᵢ, φ(i)] from a BiLSTM and attention, and scores s(i, j) = s_m(i) + s_m(j) + s_a(i, j), so mention-ness is learned jointly with coreference (slides 50–69).

Why can't the end-to-end model score every pair of spans, and what does it do instead?

There are O(T²) spans and O(T⁴) pairs. It prunes to spans with high mention scores (slide 70; D6 Problem 6).

A classmate says the span head in Lee et al. comes from a dependency parser. Correct them.

The head is learned by attention over the span's words (x̂ᵢ), not chosen by a parser (slide 51). Parser heads are used in the earlier neural coref model.

Why is clustering easier than mention-pair linking?asked

A lone pair (Google vs Google Plus) is very ambiguous. A cluster pair offers many signals: entity types, many compatible cross pairs, string similarity, better-matching context (D6 01:12:17; slide 81).

Scores: (Google, the company) 5, (Google Plus, the product) 4, (Google, Google Plus) −3. What does agglomerative clustering do?

Merge {Google, the company} and {Google Plus, the product}. Do not merge Google with Google Plus. Stop when no score is positive (slides 73–74).

Your system puts everything into one big cluster. Which B-cubed number drops, and why?

Precision, |S∩G|/|S|: each mention's system cluster is full of wrong members. Recall stays high. Splitting does the opposite.

Chains {Virat Kohli, Kohli, he}, {Rohit Sharma, the Mumbai Indians' captain, his, Sharma, his}, {the Mumbai Indians, his team}. With 13 NP mentions: 78 pairs, 14 positive, 64 negative. Ranking picks one antecedent each or NA. Full answer in D6 Problem 1 (D6 01:16:33).

Which metric would you report for each coreference model?student Q

Mention pair: binary-classifier accuracy. Mention ranking: accuracy or ranking measures/correlation. Clusters: clustering metrics such as B-cubed (D6 01:25:02).

Question answering (D7, slides only)

Name the three stages of IR-based factoid QA and the output of each.

Question processing (answer type and query) → passage retrieval (ranked passages) → answer processing (ranked candidate strings) (slides 95–96).

"Who founded Virgin Airlines?" What is the answer type, and why detect it at all?

PERSON. It eliminates candidates of the wrong type and tells the answer-extraction tagger what to look for (slide 99).

"They're the two states you could be reentering if you're crossing Florida's northern border." Give the answer type, query, focus and relation.

US state; "two states border Florida north"; "the two states"; borders(Florida, ?x, north) (slide 98).

Regex rules or a classifier for answer-type detection?

A classifier over a taxonomy (Li & Roth), with lexical, syntactic (headword) and semantic features, and the regex rules included as features. Rules alone are "not robust enough" (slides 105–108).

"Which city in China has the largest number of foreign financial companies?" How do you get the answer type without a classifier?

Take the headword of the first NP after the wh-word: city → LOCATION (slide 104).

"Who was Queen Victoria's second son?" The passage contains five PERSONs. How do you pick Alfred?

Rank candidates with features: answer-type match, keyword distance, apposition ("Alfred, the second son of Queen Victoria"), novelty, punctuation location, longest run of question terms (slides 116–118).

Three questions; first correct answers at ranks 1, 3 and none. MRR?

(1 + ⅓ + 0) / 3 = 0.444.

A user searches "tragic love story" and the right passage says "star-crossed lovers". Why does TF-IDF fail, and what fixes it?

No word overlap. Dense retrieval: encode question and passage (bi-encoder, BERT/LSTM) and score by dot product (slides 123–124).

What is a hard negative for a dense retriever, and why use one?

A top-BM25 passage that matches the question's words but lacks the answer. It teaches the model to separate lexical similarity from relevance; random and in-batch negatives are too easy (slide 128).

"Whose granddaughter starred in E.T.?" Which QA paradigm suits it, and how?

Knowledge-based or hybrid: extract the relations (acted-in ?x "E.T.") and (granddaughter-of ?x ?y) and query a relation database (slide 120).

"In 1594 he took a job as a tax collector in Andalusia." Why is Thoreau a bad candidate?

Temporal reasoning: he was born in 1817, so he was not alive in 1594. Cervantes was (slide 121).

A shop wants to know which camera feature to fix, but your model returns "3.5★". What do you change?

Move to aspect-based SA: per-aspect scores (Battery 1.5, Display 1.0, Zoom 5.0) instead of one sentence- or document-level label (slide 43).

A CNN reads bigrams of "The movie is very good". How does it get a fixed-length vector, and which bigram dominates?

Max-pooling over every feature across positions. Most of the maxima come from "very good" (slides 51–54).

"Its battery is awesome but camera is poor." A plain LSTM gives the same output for target battery and target camera. Fix?

TD-LSTM: a left LSTM up to the target plus a right LSTM from the end to the target, so the target position changes the input. TC-LSTM also feeds the target with each word (slides 60–62).

What does attention add for "battery" vs "camera", and how do AT-LSTM and ATAE-LSTM differ?

The highest weight goes to awesome for battery and poor for camera. AT adds the aspect embedding at attention only; ATAE also at the input. A multi-word aspect is averaged, (emb_battery + emb_life)/2 (slides 71–74).

Hindi review words are missing from your embedding model. What did Akhtar et al. do?

Bilingual embeddings in a shared space. Translate OOV words to English and look them up there, and use English lexicons for features. Multi-lingual: train and test in Hindi. Cross-lingual: train in English, test in Hindi (slides 64–68).

Extract the ASTE triplets from "The pizza is delicious, but the service is terrible". What would ASQP add?

(pizza, delicious, positive) and (service, terrible, negative). ASQP adds the aspect category: (food, pizza, delicious, positive) and (service, service, terrible, negative) (slides 78–82).

Why did ABSA move from pipelines to generative models?

Pipelines propagate errors between sub-tasks, and classification under-uses label semantics. One generative model outputs all the tuples (GAS). Seq2Path then removes the false ordering between tuples (slides 83–87).
